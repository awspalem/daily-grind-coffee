"Single origin" is on nearly every specialty bag now, ours included. It sounds
like a quality mark. It isn't one. It's a statement about where the coffee came
from and how tightly you can trace it.

## The ladder of traceability

- **Single origin** — one country, sometimes one region. Ethiopia Yirgacheffe is
  single origin.
- **Single estate** — one farm. Our Chikmagalur Attikan Estate Honey is single
  estate.
- **Micro-lot** — a specific pick from a specific plot, often a single day's
  harvest processed on its own. Our Araku Valley Red Honey is a micro-lot.

The further down the ladder, the more a roaster can tell you about the people,
the altitude, the variety and the processing — and usually, the more the coffee
tastes of a particular place rather than a house style.

## What single origin gets you

A single origin coffee is meant to taste *of somewhere*. Our Yirgacheffe tastes
like jasmine and peach because that's what heirloom varieties grown at 2,150m and
dried on raised beds in Gedeb taste like. Change the origin and you change the
cup completely. That's the appeal: you're drinking a place.

## When a blend is the smarter choice

Blends get a bad rap because industrial coffee hides behind the word. A
thoughtfully built blend is a different thing. Our Dawn Patrol blends washed
Colombian and natural Brazilian specifically so it:

- tastes consistent bag to bag, all year
- works in a drip machine, French press or pour-over without fuss
- holds up to milk

If you want one coffee you make on autopilot every morning, a blend is often the
better buy. Save the single origins for the brew you actually sit down with.

## How to use this when you shop

Check the roast meter and tasting notes first — those tell you how it'll taste.
Then check the origin detail for how much the roaster actually knows about the
lot. Lots of specifics is a good sign. Vague ones aren't a dealbreaker, but they
tell you you're buying a flavour, not a story.
